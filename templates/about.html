About StarExplorer

Explore the real stellar neighbourhood, investigate plausible planetary populations, and build fictional worlds on an astronomical foundation.

What it can do Astronomical databases Statistical modelling Snapshots & scenarios Limits & interpretation Sources & references

What is this tool?

StarExplorer is an interactive three-dimensional map built with a Python Flask server and a three.js/WebGL browser renderer. It combines a catalogue of roughly 107,000 stars with neutron stars, black holes, nebulae, and a distant backdrop of galaxies and galaxy clusters. Positions are expressed in parsecs, with Sol at the origin; one parsec is about 3.26 light-years.

  • Rotate, pan, zoom, search by name or catalogue ID, select objects, and centre the view on stars or Galactic objects. Touch controls and responsive panels support phones and tablets.
  • Switch between Galactic and equatorial J2000 coordinates, use axis-aligned views, and inspect distances, spectral classes, catalogue identifiers and source notes.
  • Filter by distance, object type, spectral class, sovereignty or planetary category. Combine nested ALL, ANY and NOT rules and save or share filter text.
  • Inspect planetary systems, orbit diagrams, stellar properties, habitable zones, planet descriptions and moons. Switch published scenarios to explore alternative populations.
  • Colour stars by their spectral colours, planetary categories or fictional sovereignties. Add an optional seeded volumetric subspace-disturbance layer.

The map is a spatial exploration and world-building tool, not a telescope image or a precision astronomical measurement service. The separate User guide in Help explains the controls step by step.

How astronomical databases were used

The catalogue supplies the observational foundation. Import tools convert source records into local JSON datasets, which the server validates and serves to the browser. The app does not query an astronomical archive every time you move the camera.

Stars: HYG Database v4.0
Most stars come from David Nash's HYG compilation; the initial ten entries were entered by hand. The import uses usable distances and spectral classes, preserves existing entries, and suppresses close companions within 0.05 pc of an included star to avoid overlapping map points. Colours are derived from B−V colour indices. Names and catalogue identifiers make the map searchable.
Confirmed planets: NASA Exoplanet Archive
The pscomppars table is downloaded and host stars are cross-matched to the map using catalogue identifiers and, where needed, sky position and distance. Positional matching allows a small angular separation and a distance tolerance; it is not just a name lookup. Sol has a separate Solar System dataset. Available archive stellar parameters take precedence over estimates derived from the map's spectral data.
Nebulae and molecular clouds
Curated literature entries are combined with planetary-nebula distances and morphology from González-Santamaría et al. (2021), using Gaia EDR3 parallaxes, and local molecular-cloud sightlines from Zucker et al. (2020). Position, physical size and morphology guide the rendering; the cloud textures themselves are procedural illustrations.
Compact objects and galaxies
Curated neutron-star, black-hole and extragalactic catalogues use published positions, distances and other properties, with notes about individual objects. Neutron-star positions use SIMBAD/ATNF information or coordinates encoded in J2000 object names. These lists supplement the star catalogue; they are not complete surveys of every object.

How statistical techniques fill the gaps

Most catalogue stars do not have a measured planetary system. Rather than treating every unmeasured star as planet-free, the generator makes a synthetic population using occurrence-rate models informed by exoplanet research. This is Monte Carlo sampling: distributions describe a population, and seeded random draws produce one plausible realisation. It is not a detection or a prediction of the actual planets around a particular star.

  1. Estimate stellar properties. Spectral type, luminosity class and absolute magnitude inform temperature, luminosity, mass and radius estimates, using a stellar sequence based on Pecaut & Mamajek. These estimates are inputs to the planet model, not new measurements.
  2. Choose which populations occur. Probabilistic host selection depends on stellar group and evolutionary state; giant-planet occurrence also depends on stellar mass and sampled metallicity. Metallicity is drawn from a Gaussian distribution when simulated, rather than assumed to have been measured for each star.
  3. Draw planet counts and properties. Populations use host probabilities and one-plus-Poisson multiplicities, not a fixed number of planets for every star. Piecewise power-law distributions supply periods, sizes or masses. Log-normal scatter gives related but non-identical planet sizes within a system and variation in inferred masses. Empirical relations connect mass and radius.
  4. Model eccentricities and reject implausible arrangements. Rayleigh, Gaussian and beta distributions are used for different planet populations. Very short-period orbits are circularised in the model. Crossing or overly close orbits are removed using a mutual-Hill-radius spacing rule. This is a plausibility screen, not a long-term gravitational simulation or proof of stability.
  5. Calculate derived properties. Orbital periods, insolation, equilibrium temperatures, habitable-zone membership and approximate tidal-locking behaviour follow physical formulae and published prescriptions. Moon populations use host-mass and Hill-sphere constraints plus statistical sampling; simulated exomoons are not observations.

Small-planet occurrence draws on SAG13 and Dressing & Charbonneau; giant planets on Johnson, Fulton and Cumming; cold Neptunes on Suzuki. The radius valley and within-system size similarity are inspired by Fulton and Weiss. Eccentricity and mass–radius models draw on Kipping, Van Eylen, and Chen & Kipping. These are simplified, research-informed prescriptions, not full reproductions of every cited study or a fitted posterior for each star.

Randomness is deterministic per seed and star name for a fixed generator and input version. Re-running that same setup recreates its generated population. Changing the seed explores a different realisation; changing the input data or generator can also change the result.

Why there are databases and published scenarios

Generation happens offline. A baseline freezes the full planetary population, including empty systems and moon lists, into an indexed SQLite database. Metadata records the seed, generator version, source hashes and catalogue fingerprint. The web app reads these published databases without regenerating planets during requests.

A named scenario pins a baseline and can replace selected systems with explicitly authored planets and moons. Publishing validates replacements, recalculates derived properties and creates an immutable revision. Unchanged systems inherit their baseline. Manually altered physical properties and newly authored bodies carry manual provenance rather than silently becoming confirmed observations.

The browser first receives compact planet counts and category flags in catalogue order, then requests detailed systems as needed. This keeps the initial map smaller than sending every planetary system and moon. Revision identifiers and catalogue fingerprints help prevent mixing incompatible data. SQLite snapshots must be deployed and backed up separately from the editable JSON; they are excluded from Git.

Reading the map responsibly

  • Observed, simulated and manual are different. A confirmed planet may still have estimated parameters and simulated moons. A generated planet is a model outcome. A manual scenario system is authored content. Check provenance and source notes.
  • Potentially habitable does not mean inhabited. The flag uses simplified size/mass, stellar and habitable-zone criteria. Equilibrium temperature is not surface temperature; atmospheres, climate, water and biology are not established by this model.
  • The star sample is incomplete and biased. HYG is magnitude-limited: distant faint stars, especially red dwarfs, are missing. Parallax uncertainties increase with distance. Counts and percentages on this map should not be read as unbiased Galactic population estimates.
  • Rendering is illustrative. Point sizes and compact-object glyphs aid visibility, not physical scale. Nebula detail and orientation are not reconstructed images. Distant galaxy positions and curated literature values have uncertainties.
  • Subspace disturbance and sovereignties are fictional. The disturbance is a seeded mathematical field, not measured interstellar gas or a discovered physical phenomenon. Its glow accumulates along the line of sight; its local percentage is a dimensionless model strength, not a calibrated scientific measurement.
